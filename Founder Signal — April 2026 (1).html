Section VI

Positioning Playbook

Seven moves — derived from what this cohort got right, what it converged on too quickly, and where the structural gaps remain open.

01
Lead with the insight, not the credential

Eight of ten founders in this cohort open with who they are. Investors have read fifty "ex-Unit 8200 founders" decks this month. The founders who break through open with the insight nobody else has articulated yet.

The template is Capsule: "AI agents are a new class of privileged user." That sentence did not exist before Paz said it. One sentence that creates a new mental model is your opening — not your prior employer.

  • Write the insight in one sentence that makes the listener think they have not heard it framed that way before
  • Your credential should validate the insight, not precede it
  • If your strongest line is your pedigree, you have not found the insight yet
Test: Can someone who does not know you repeat your insight at a meeting the next morning? If not, it is not sharp enough.
02
Specify the timing window with a data point

Every founder uses urgency framing. Most do it vaguely — "AI is advancing rapidly" carries no information. The ones that close tier-1 rounds cite a specific event or measurement from the last twelve months that explains why the window is open now and will close.

Artemis cites the CrowdStrike dwell time report. Pillar cites the 2025 commodity volatility spike. Q-Factor cites the neutral-atom breakthrough that makes million-qubit systems feasible for the first time. Each is specific, recent, and verifiable.

  • Identify the one data point or event that changed the equation in the last twelve months
  • Name the technical or economic unlock: what is structurally possible now that was not before
  • Name the consequence of waiting: what closes or gets captured by competitors in 18 months
Template: "[Event] in [date] changed [dynamic]. This means [unlock] is now achievable. Organisations that act within [window] capture [advantage]. Those that wait face [consequence]."
03
Find the second-order problem

The most overcrowded position in any hot market is the obvious first-order problem. Five companies in this cohort target cybersecurity detection. Three target AI ROI and data infrastructure. The companies creating genuinely new categories attacked one level above these.

Fig does not solve attacks — it solves broken defences. Capsule does not secure applications — it secures AI agents operating inside applications. Hilbert does not provide AI — it provides the data infrastructure that makes AI work. In each case, the second-order problem is less contested, more specific, and has cleaner ROI.

  • Ask: what breaks because of the first-order problem that nobody is currently addressing?
  • Ask: what new problem is created when the first-order solution is adopted? That is often the highest-value wedge.
  • The second-order problem typically has shorter procurement cycles — the buyer can see the cost of not solving it immediately
04
Your wedge is a workflow, not a feature

Every company in this cohort that closed a fast round owns a specific workflow on day one — not a capability. Pillar owns the hedging execution workflow. Above owns the insider investigation workflow. Artemis owns the alert-triage-and-response workflow.

A feature is "we detect anomalies." A workflow is "when an anomaly is detected, we automatically contain it, generate an incident narrative, and log it for compliance — without a human touching it." The workflow is what you price. The feature is what enables it.

  • Map the specific workflow your buyer executes manually today, step by step
  • Identify the highest-cost or highest-risk step — that is your entry point
  • Build toward owning the end-to-end workflow; enter at the step where ROI is immediate and undeniable
05
Compress the seed-to-A arc with revenue proof

Above Security: eight months old, ten employees, $43M Series A. Artemis: multiple seven-figure contracts closed before announcing. The playbook has shifted. The current structure is: seed → sell immediately → raise A on revenue evidence. Not: seed → build → find customers → raise A.

The investor language changed in 2026. Product-market fit is necessary but no longer sufficient. The current requirement is revenue with low-friction acquisition — demonstrated, not projected.

  • Identify three to five design partners who will pay a nominal amount from day one
  • Build the minimum viable workflow that produces measurable ROI — not the full vision
  • Document every customer outcome in dollar terms from week one. That documentation is your Series A case
Current bar: By the time you announce a seed publicly, the narrative should include: "We have been generating revenue for N months." That sentence restructures every subsequent investor conversation.
06
Position as infrastructure, not tooling

Hilbert's a16z investment thesis is: LLMs are commoditised, the infrastructure layer underneath is the moat. That framing justifies infrastructure multiples (8–12× ARR) rather than tool multiples (3–5×). Pillar applies the same logic to financial risk — once embedded, it owns the execution substrate beneath every commodity decision the company makes.

The test: does your product become more valuable as more of the customer's data and workflows live in it? If yes, that is infrastructure — position, price, and sell it accordingly.

  • Infrastructure contracts are sold to CFOs and CTOs, not department heads — know which room you are targeting
  • Infrastructure pricing is per-seat, percentage of flow, or ARR — not per-query or per-feature
  • The infrastructure framing requires demonstrating compounding value over time, not just feature completeness
07
The structural gap: operator-founders outside cybersecurity

Seven of ten launches in this cohort are cybersecurity or AI infrastructure. That is where pedigree is most legible, investor conviction is highest, and deal velocity is fastest. It is also where competition is most acute.

The operator-founder template — deep institutional expertise plus direct experience as the underserved customer — is the most underrepresented formation pattern in this cohort. Pillar is the only example. It raised at a16z with the Uber CEO at seed. The template is validated and the replication opportunity is wide open across non-financial verticals.

  • Verticals with structurally identical conditions to Pillar: SME manufacturing, logistics, agriculture, energy trading, professional services billing, healthcare operations, legal workflow
  • The formula: spend five or more years inside the problem at institutional level, understand both why the institutional solution exists and why the SME cannot access it, build the autonomous access layer
  • You do not need intelligence unit credentials. You need to have been the customer — operationally, with capital at risk
The open position for the remainder of 2026: Build the Pillar equivalent for a non-financial vertical where autonomous execution is newly viable — industrial supply chain, energy markets, professional services billing. All three have the same market structure. None have comparable venture competition.
